The consultant has completed a preliminary hydraulic study and has made recommendations
for improvements to this area known as East Park.  The preliminary recommendations are
summarized below and will be finalized with this design.

1. Construct an engineered berm north of the existing UPRR embankment. This additional
berm will protect the existing railroad embankment by removing storm and flood waters
and eliminating pooling and erosion of the existing railroad embankment.
2. Install 3 – 60” borings thru the existing UPRR embankment and the constructed
engineered berm referenced in Item No. 1 above, following all AREMA Guidelines. These
three pipes will convey the storm water from East Park thru the embankment to
continue flowing in Dry Creek south of the embankment.
3. Install 1 – 54” boring thru the existing UPRR embankment near the existing arch
structure also following all AREMA Guidelines.  This 54” boring will convey storm water
that is currently flowing along the UPRR embankment from the west and east, directing
it to the south into Dry Creek on the south side of the UPRR embankment.

Reason for Grant:

Over the last 120-years, the Dry Creek basin has experienced 100-year or larger flood events at
least seven times.  Four of those resulted in significant impounding of water against the UPRR
embankment referenced in this letter.  There have been at least four occasions where as much
as 19-feet of stormwater has been impounded against the UPRR embankment since the 6’ x 8’
box arch culvert was constructed in 1903.  During the last major event in 1985, the 6’ x 8’ box
arch culvert was partially blocked with flood debris.  On average, our community experiences a
large magnitude event in the Dry Creek drainage basin about every twenty years.  It is not a
matter of if but when the next big flood event will occur in the basin. We have not experienced
that level of storm in some time.

Clearly the recommended improvements provide protection of UPRR facilities and removes the
primary Dry Creek Basin storm water from utilizing the historic box arch structure.   We believe
these recommendations provide direct benefit to the UPRR and an opportunity for the City of
Cheyenne to potentially use the historic box arch structure as a pedestrian underpass for the
Cheyenne Greenway system.

Cost and Funding:

The total cost of project to the City of Cheyenne shall not exceed $50,000.  The funding for the
project is the 2019-2022 5th Penny 1% Sales Tax dedicated Drainage Fund.

The City of Cheyenne, Wyoming is applying for FY2020 Building Resilient Infrastructure and Communities (BRIC) Project Scoping funding under the Capability-and
Capacity- Building (C&CB) Activity for funding assistance for a project scoping/design effort in the lower Dry Creek drainage basin. Our proposed project scoping
effort/design will evaluate and identify a feasible, constructible boring location under the Union Pacific Railroad main line tracks entering into the eastern corporate
limits of Cheyenne. This project scoping effort will address a critical flood hazard for the community and result in an application-ready construction project for a
future BRIC funding opportunity in FY2022. The City of Cheyenne, established in 1867 as a railhead along the Union Pacific Railroad, was built along Crow Creek.
As Cheyenne grew, its growth occurred away from Crow Creek and along the nine tributaries within the corporate limits including the Dry Creek drainage basin.
Dry Creek is a tributary of Crow Creek with a drainage area of 14.6 sq. miles. The headwaters originate on Francis E. Warren AFB. The north and south forks of Dry
Creek flow through the Western Hills subdivision and form a confluence into the main branch just east of I-25 on School District property. Much of the creek has
been infringed upon as the community developed resulting in loss of floodplain and increasing drainage problems. In the community’s 1988 Dry Creek Drainage
Master Plan, flood problems were classified as life-safety hazards and/or property damage hazards. Commercial and residential areas upstream of the Union
Pacific Railroad (UPRR) embankment in the lower basin was classified as a high life-safety hazard area. So was Campstool Road, downstream of I-80. Since 1988,
commercial and residential development has grown exponentially both upstream and downstream of the UPRR and, the channel downstream of the Campstool Rd.
crossing, between Campstool Rd. and I-80, has continued to degrade. An ongoing head cut is affecting the Campstool Rd. crossing, approximately 1000-feet
downstream of the UPRR. Floods with the highest peak discharges affecting Cheyenne are the result of intense convective rainstorms occurring over the
community. The earliest flood of record was in 1883 and, the most devastating occurred in 1985. The 1985 event produced record peak discharges throughout the
Dry Creek basin resulting in overtopping of every road crossing and multiple deaths along the Sheridan Reach of Dry Creek. At the UPRR, the ensuing floodwaters
became ponded to a depth of 20-feet behind the 25-foot high railroad embankment, nearly overtopping it. The ponded waters inundated rural residents and a

with Crow Creek. Floods of the 1985 magnitude have occurred in the lower Dry Creek basin in 1918, 1935, and 1955. The 1955 event produced similar depths of

more than a 10-year flood event. The embankment begins to backup and pond water beginning with a 10-year event. The City is experiencing significant growth in
the lower Dry Creek basin including continued commercial growth in the LEEDS business park, immediately downstream of the UPRR. The continued potential for
significant ponding at the UPRR embankment is a dangerous situation that impacts development in the lower Dry Creek basin and presents a critical life-safety
hazard for businesses and traveling public downstream of the UPRR. The City of Cheyenne is pursuing the development of a feasible and constructible boring
