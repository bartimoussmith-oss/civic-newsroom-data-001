Dear Mr. Quattrini:

Trihydro Corporation appreciates the opportunity to provide this cost proposal to the City of Cheyenne
(City) for the 2024 environmental monitoring at the Cheyenne Sanitary Landfill (Landfill).  This proposal
is for a one-year extension of the existing Happy Jack Landfill Environmental Monitoring Contract,
#6798, between the City of Cheyenne and Trihydro Corporation, set to expire on March 1, 2024.  Work
will fulfill requirements permitted under the Sanitary Landfill Environmental Monitoring Plan (SLEMP)
originally dated April 12, 2017, and revised March 14, 2019.

SCOPE OF WORK
Our project understanding and the scope of work for each proposed task is outlined below for your review
and consideration.  The scope of work was developed based on our experience conducting the
environmental monitoring at the Landfill since 2002 and from our experience working with the Wyoming
Department of Environmental Quality, Solid & Hazardous Waste Division (WDEQ/SHWD) on dozens of
sites across Wyoming.  A detailed cost estimate for each task and a description of related activities and
assumptions is provided as Attachment A.

Task 1A and 2A – April and August Environmental Monitoring
Semiannual groundwater monitoring will be conducted in April and August whereby static water levels
will be gauged in up to 40 wells and 2 leachate sumps, and groundwater samples collected from up to
36 wells and 1 leachate sump.  Samples will be submitted for laboratory analysis of the Wyoming Solid
Waste Rules and Regulations (WSWRR), Chapter 2, Appendix A, B, and/or C constituents, as designated
by the SLEMP.  Methane monitoring of structures and perimeter wells will also be conducted during
these semiannual events.

Our 2024 cost estimate for the April and August semiannual monitoring events is $59,283, which
includes an estimated $28,875 for laboratory services.  Despite our aggressive and beneficial negotiations

agreed to honor last year’s quote through 2024, including a flat rate for standard overnight shipping at
$60/cooler.  Many laboratories are no longer providing pre-paid shipping due to the erratic market.  If
Eurofins had not extended their shipping agreement, shipping costs would have increased by at least
$190/cooler, or $4,750 per year (assuming 25 coolers).  We are pleased that our negotiations are resulting
in a substantial cost savings to the City.

Task 1B and 2B – April and August Environmental Reporting
Semiannual reports will be prepared for the April and August environmental monitoring events and
include data management, data validation, and report preparation.  Reports will provide a narrative of
field, laboratory, validation activities, and results as well as observations, conclusions, and
recommendations.  The report will also provide relevant tables, figures, and appendices, as outlined in
Attachment A.  Our 2024 cost estimate for the April and August semiannual reporting is $14,320.

Task 3 – Two Quarterly Methane Monitoring Events
Under Task 3, two quarterly methane monitoring events will be conducted in February and December.
During each event, 14 perimeter methane probes, five passive gas vents, and four structures (scale house,
office, shop area, and equipment building) will be visited to measure and verify that methane
concentrations are within permissible limits.  (The other two quarterly events will be covered under Tasks
1A and 1B, in April and August.)  Our 2024 cost estimate for this task is $4,006.

Task 4 – Annual Environmental Reporting
Following collection of all data required for monitoring during 2024, an Annual Report will be prepared
to summarize findings over the course of the year.  Statistical analysis of groundwater in each of the
three water-bearing zones will also be conducted under this task.  Our proposed budget for this task is
$11,633, including approximately $4,800 for the statistical analysis of the three water-bearing zones.

INTRODUCTION
This Sanitary Landfill Environmental Monitoring Plan (SLEMP) has been prepared to address groundwater, leachate,
and methane monitoring requirements at the City of Cheyenne Sanitary Landfill, also known as the Happy Jack
Landfill (Landfill). The plan has been prepared as part of the facility’s 2017 lifetime permit application, primarily to
fulfill monitoring requirements contained within the Wyoming Department of Environmental Quality/Solid and
Hazardous Waste Division (WDEQ/SHWD) municipal solid waste landfill regulations. Additional monitoring
requirements have also been incorporated pursuant to the WDEQ November 25, 2015 Location Standards Variance
Approval for the expansion area. This SLEMP has been prepared in accordance with revised Chapter 2 of the
WDEQ/SHWD Rules and Regulations dated January 3, 2017.
The primary objective of the environmental monitoring program at the Landfill is early identification of impacts to the
subsurface as a result of landfill activities. Additionally, the hydrogeologic and analytical data generated during routine
monitoring may be used for assessment and/or remedial design purposes, if impacts are identified in subsurface soils or
groundwater beneath the landfill.
The remainder of the SLEMP is organized into the following sections:
Section 2.0— Groundwater and Leachate Monitoring Plan: provides a description for each of the aquifers beneath
the landfill, leachate monitoring points, different phases of monitoring, frequency of sampling, as well as fluid
gauging and groundwater sampling procedures. This section also addresses the off-site water well monitoring
program for wells within a half mile of the expansion area boundary that are permitted or certificated for domestic
or stock use.
•
Section 3.0— Groundwater Data Quality Assurance/Quality Control: presents the quality assurance objectives for
this project and the methodology for reviewing field and laboratory data.
•
Section 4.0— Groundwater Data Statistical Analyses: includes a description of the statistical methods used to
evaluate the groundwater analytical results from each monitoring event.
•
Section 5.0
— Landfill Methane Monitoring Plan: describes the monitoring system, frequency, and procedures for
monitoring methane in landfill structures and in the subsurface.
•
Section 6.0
— Notification Requirements: outlines the notification requirements and follow up actions to
statistically significant increases in contaminant concentrations in groundwater, detections above groundwater
protection standards, and methane concentration measuring more than 25% or 100% of the lower explosive limit
(LEL).

2.2.4
SEMI-CONFINED AQUIFER
In 2013, a series of groundwater monitoring wells (GMW series) was installed to help define the subsurface
hydrogeology specifically in the landfill expansion area. WDEQ asked for confirmation as to whether the GMW-series
wells are confined and if they monitor the same water bearing zone as the C-series wells, a set of wells that had been
described as monitoring a confined zone within the Ogallala Aquifer identified when the wells were drilled in 1999.
The confined zone was said to be primarily composed of sand with minor amounts of clay, silt, and gravel which lies
below the Principal Unconfined Aquifer unit at approximately 170 ft-bgs.
A transducer study was then completed to shed more light on the local subsurface hydrology as a condition of the
WDEQ Location Standards Variance Approval. The results of the study indicated that the Confined Aquifer would be
more appropriately called a Semi-Confined aquifer. The wells monitor different intervals but may represent a series of
“stacked” water bearing zones that locally are connected to varying degrees. Some may meet the definition of confined
(C-series) because water levels in certain wells are consistently above the screened zone, but the wells as a group do
not appear to represent confined aquifer conditions. The Ogallala Aquifer in this part of the country is very complex
and the degree of interconnectivity is difficult to accurately assess. It was recommended that the GMW and C series
wells be plotted together to represent groundwater flow conditions below the Principal Unconfined Aquifer set of
wells. USGS mapping shows a general northeasterly groundwater flow gradient that begins to change to the northwest
consistent with what is also observed in the potentiometric surface maps constructed based on the C-series and
GMW-series wells.
Approximately ten (10) municipal water wells operated by the Cheyenne Board of Public Utilities (BOPU) are located
within a one-mile radius of the landfill site. These wells are installed at depths between 200 and 400 ft-bgs. Pumping
from the wells operated by the Board of Public Utilities is intermittent as necessary to augment the City of Cheyenne’s
water supply. Each of the water supply wells has the capacity to produce between 150 to 600 gallons per minute from
the Ogallala Aquifer. The transducer study results indicated that even at high late-summer pumping rates, the BOPU
pumping wells did not alter the aquifer flow direction from the regional flow pattern. The Cheyenne Board of Public
Utilities intermittently collects groundwater samples from these wells and analyzes the samples for the Appendix A
constituent list. This data is provided to the City and shared with WDEQ through the annual report. Three (3) C-series
and 5 GMW-series wells will be gauged and sampled in April and August of each year according to the detection
monitoring program (Tables 3 and 4).

2.3
GROUNDWATER MONITORING PROCEDURES
This section details the procedures and techniques that will be followed for groundwater monitoring at the Landfill.
Due to the heterogeneity of the hydrogeologic units encountered beneath the landfill, it may be necessary to modify
these procedures as sampling progresses to ensure that representative groundwater samples are collected and submitted
to the laboratory for analysis.
2.3.1
FLUID LEVEL MONITORING
Upon arriving at a well location, sampling personnel will check the well for damage and remove the lock and well cap.
Any damage to the wellhead or casing, or any items requiring maintenance will be noted in the field sampling record
and reported to the project manager so that the necessary repairs can be scheduled. Field personnel will wear new,
sterile gloves during all preparation and sampling activities.
Fluid levels within each water bearing zone will be measured within a 24-hour period during the two scheduled
sampling events (April and August). Fluid level measurements will be performed using a water level indicator accurate
to 0.01-feet. The fluid level probe will be lowered down the center of the well casing. When the probe encounters
water, an audio alarm is triggered. The measurement will be made from the pre-marked (surveyed) measuring point on
the north side of the well casing. Manufacturer’s instructions will be followed to ensure proper care of the fluid level
probe. The total depth of the well will be measured annually to determine if the well is silting in and needs to be
rehabilitated or replaced.
If non-aqueous phase liquid (NAPL) is observed in any well, the thickness will be measured with an interface probe. A
typical interface probe gives an audible beeping sound when submerged in water and a solid tone when submerged in
NAPL. The fluid level measurements will be recorded on the fluid level summary form (or similar) included in
Attachment B.
All measurements during a particular monitoring event are made to the reference points with the same instrument, to
ensure internal consistency. The vertical reference points (i.e. north side top of casing) are established by a licensed
surveyor or under supervision of a professional engineer in relation to the nearest permanent benchmark. The
horizontal well coordinates are established by marking distances in reference to the project site plan in triangulation or
by surveying in reference to the nearest permanent benchmark. The exposed portion of the tape and the probe will be
decontaminated before performing measurements at each monitoring well.
