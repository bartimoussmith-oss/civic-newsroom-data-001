Purpose
In accordance with the Cheyenne/Laramie County Emergency Operations Plan, this annex serves
the as the framework for responding to an earthquake emergency. This annex establishes a
comprehensive format outlining proactive measures to be executed before, during, and
immediately following a seismic event. The outlined activities within this plan are designed to
facilitate a coordinated and effective response, ensuring the safety and well-being of the
community while minimizing the impact of the earthquake. This document aims to provide
emergency personnel with a strategic guide to navigate the complexities of an earthquake
scenario, emphasizing the importance of preparedness, response coordination, and swift action
in the face of such a crisis.
Nature of the Event
In coordination with the Cheyenne/Laramie County Emergency Operations Plan, it is imperative
to comprehend the nature and potential ramifications of an earthquake event. Described as a
sudden, violent shaking or movement of the Earth's surface, this phenomenon arises from the
abrupt displacement of rock masses, typically occurring within the upper 10 to 20 miles of the
Earth's surface.
The seismic hazards associated with earthquakes are multifaceted and include the following
elements:
1. Ground Motion: This encompasses the vibration and shaking of the ground during an
earthquake, constituting the most far-reaching effect that inflicts significant damage to
buildings, structures, and infrastructure.
2. Ground Surface Fault Rupture: The ground shaking results from the rupture of a fault
beneath the surface. This rupture can lead to the surface of the ground opening up, with
potential openings of up to 20 feet, intensifying the impact of the seismic event.
3. Liquefaction: This phenomenon entails the temporary loss of ground strength, causing
the ground to behave as a viscous fluid akin to quicksand, rather than a solid structure.
4. Landslides: Earthquakes can trigger landslides, involving rock falls and the sliding of rock
fragments on steep slopes, thereby adding to the overall geological risks.
5. Secondary Hazards: Consequences of earthquakes may extend beyond the seismic
event itself and encompass secondary hazards such as fire outbreaks, hazardous material
releases, or structural failures. Recognizing and addressing these potential secondary
hazards is integral to a comprehensive emergency management response.
Situation and Assumptions
Situation
Considering the unique seismic situation in Laramie County, where earth tremors are infrequent
but not entirely unheard of, it is essential to acknowledge the broader context of seismic activity
in the region. While the western part of Wyoming experiences several tremors annually, Laramie
County itself has encountered sporadic instances of small earthquakes or tremors over the past
century, with no reported damage.

Despite the relatively low frequency of seismic events, it is crucial for Emergency Responders to
recognize that Laramie County is not exempt from the potential impact of earthquakes. The United
States Geological Survey highlights the active ground movement area associated with the
Yellowstone Caldera, introducing an additional layer of consideration for emergency
preparedness.
Although earthquake risk is not classified as high, the potential consequences of a moderate to
extreme quake could pose a significant threat to the area's infrastructure. Therefore, measures
must be in place to address the specific challenges posed by earthquakes, ensuring a proactive
and effective response to minimize potential devastation and safeguard the community and its
infrastructure.
Assumptions
1. Seismic activities follow certain patterns based on historical records.
2. Earthquakes will come with no warning.
3. Certain types of buildings and infrastructure may be more vulnerable to earthquake
damage.
4. High population density can lead to increased casualties and challenges in evacuation.
5. Earthquakes may disrupt communication infrastructure.
6. Earthquakes may trigger secondary hazards such as landslides, tsunamis (in coastal
areas), or aftershocks.
7. Effective coordination among various agencies and organizations is crucial.
8. Earthquake recovery can be a prolonged and complex process.

Dam Failure/Causes
Dam failures can result from various causes, including overtopping, foundation defects, piping
and seepage failures, and conduit and valve issues. Overtopping may occur due to poor spillway
design, settling of the dam crest, or spillway blockage, leading to high water levels during heavy
rainfall. Foundation defects result from settling in the dam foundation, slope instability, uplift
pressures, and seepage, causing structural instability. Piping and seepage failures involve
internal erosion from seepage, erosion along hydraulic structures, animal burrows, and dam
structure cracks. Conduit and valve failures stem from problems with dam valves and conduits.
Other dam failures may arise from miscellaneous causes, such as earthquakes, landslides,
extreme storms, heavy snow-melt, equipment malfunction, structural damage, or sabotage. Dam
incidents are categorized into Type 1 (slowly developing), Type 2 (rapidly developing), and Type
3 (instantaneous failure). A Type 3 failure requires immediate notification and evacuation due to
the imminent threat, with a short time before water starts to rise.
In the specific context of Crystal or Granite Reservoirs, a dam breach could result in water
reaching Cheyenne within 3-5 hours, necessitating urgent emergency response and evacuation
procedures.

Situation
Description / Definition
In various emergency scenarios, such as floods, hazardous material spills, radiological threats,
volcanic activity, major fires, dam breaches, or similar incidents, Laramie County emergency
responders and EOC personnel may decide to evacuate all or part of the community to minimize
potential loss of life.
Regardless of the specific threat, the evacuation approach remains consistent. The initial step
involves identifying the at-risk area and weighing the risks of evacuation against those of
sheltering in place. Managers then designate routes to low-risk areas, arrange transportation for
those without private means, open and staff buildings for housing and feeding evacuees, and
provide clear instructions to the public.
Historically, during emergencies with advance warning, over 50% of residents in threatened areas
evacuate before official orders. Studies suggest that 80% of evacuees prefer seeking shelter with
friends or relatives over designated public facilities. If this trend continues, Laramie County may
need to accommodate up to 17,000 people in case of a total evacuation.
For this plan, evacuation involves removing individuals from the threat's path before impact, falling
under the jurisdiction of law enforcement and public safety as outlined in this plan. Rescue, on
the other hand, involves moving victims from an impacted area and follows departmental standard
operating procedures.
The authority to order an evacuation lies with local government or the governor, with the elected
chief executive officers in Laramie County holding the formal authority. In on-scene operations,
the Incident Commander (IC) assumes this authority. According to Wyoming statute, no one can
force citizens to evacuate against their will unless they obstruct emergency operations. However,
officials can enforce evacuation of public facilities or businesses, and law enforcement can take
action against those violating established perimeters or interfering with responders.

The Nuclear/Radiological Incident Annex aims to serve as a comprehensive guide tailored for
local governments and agencies, facilitating effective coordination and providing essential
guidelines for public safety personnel engaged in preparedness activities. This strategic
document specifically addresses Nuclear/Radiological Incidents, encompassing accidents,
terrorism, and sabotage. Additionally, it establishes a framework for seamless collaboration
between state agencies and local jurisdictions in responding to incidents involving radioactive
materials. The Annex delineates clear responsibilities and ensures the coordination of local
agencies within their jurisdictions, fostering collaboration with neighboring jurisdictions for a
cohesive and efficient response to incidents involving radioactive materials.
Scope
The local response to a specific incident is contingent upon various factors such as the local
resources' capacity for response, the type and quantity of materials involved, the potential impact
on the environment, and the size of the affected area. In the event of an incident, licensees, local
jurisdictions, and the State of Wyoming share responsibilities concerning response and recovery
efforts. This Annex serves as a dynamic framework to guide decision-making and actions,
ensuring a well-coordinated and effective response to Nuclear/Radiological Incidents.
Shipper/Carrier/Licensee
The shipper, carrier, and licensee must be in compliance with all federal, state, and local
regulations. They are responsible for notifying proper authorities of an incident, providing
expertise and shipping information to public safety and response personnel; providing equipment
and personnel to conduct clean-up and recovery of the incident site; and reimburse response
agencies as provided by law.

The “local jurisdiction” is defined as that local governmental entity which authorizes, regulates, or
is otherwise deemed responsible for the health, welfare, and protection of residents and their
property within the geographical boundaries in which a radioactive material incident occurs.

Local emergency management personnel, law enforcement officials, fire department officials, and
emergency medical technicians/paramedics will provide their usual range of emergency services

Incident Type and Classification
Incident Type:  For the purpose of this guide, there are three types of incidents involving
radioactive materials. They are:

Transportation Incident:  An incident which occurs in association with any activity
involving the movement of radioactive materials by a motorized conveyance on roadways,
to include trucks, trains, plains, automobiles, etc.  (This does not include movement of
materials at a facility by forklift, hand-truck or other transfer method. Such an incident
would be considered a fixed facility incident.)

Fixed Facility Incident:  An incident which occurs in association with any activity involving
radioactive materials at a fixed location. This would include industrial sites (uranium
processing), temporary work sites (oil well injections, non-destructive testing of welds,
etc.), manufacturing sites (thickness gauges, etc.), or any other location which does not
involve the movement of radioactive materials by a motorized conveyance on roadways
as described in #1a above.

Terrorism Incident:  An incident which occurs in association with any deliberate act of
sabotage or destruction which includes the use of radioactive materials. This type of
incident may include transportation or fixed facility but, due to the initiating event, will
require coordination of response actions to ensure crime scene issues are considered.
Incident Classification:  There are also three classifications for incidents involving radioactive
materials. They apply to each type of incident described above and are as follows:

Level I: An incident in which no release of radioactive material has occurred. This is
determined by visual assessment of the incident scene. If there is not a high confidence
level by the response personnel in declaring a Level I incident, it should default to a Level
II incident.

Level II:  An incident in which there may be a release of radioactive materials. This is
determined by visual assessment of the incident scene. Level II would be declared when
there is reasonable doubt to the integrity of the containment of the radioactive materials
(package shows significant damage, but there is no visible sign of material release).

Level III:  An incident in which there is a release of radioactive materials. This is
determined by visual assessment of the incident scene. There is a high level of confidence
by the response personnel in declaring a Level III incident.

Level of Closure Description
Laramie County is divided into three (3) areas closing in on the City of Cheyenne (see map).

closer to interior areas. As conditions deteriorate an Advisory for Level 2 and Closure for Level 1
would follow.

On rare occasions, the county has a significant amount of snow and blizzard conditions, which

efforts and coordinate response assets throughout the City and County.
