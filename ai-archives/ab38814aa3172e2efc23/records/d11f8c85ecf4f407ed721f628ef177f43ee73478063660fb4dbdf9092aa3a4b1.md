Cheyenne’s history is rooted in agriculture and the railroad, bringing together hard-
2

reclaiming this historic gathering point to chart a new economic course in the heart of

viewing pleasure, the Old Cheyenne Elevator is poised to emerge as a vibrant
community and entrepreneurial hub.
Reminiscent of the once thriving grain elevator we have embraced the name painted
on the building. “Ful-O-Pep” LLC purchased the property in late 2016 and since that
time has harnessed the energy of the space and built on the momentum of bootstrap
inspiration. In three short years, Ful-O-Pep revitalized and renovated two other
warehouse properties onsite that are now home to Blossom Yoga and Hammock Fine
Art. We have now set our sights on The Old Cheyenne Elevator; the keystone building
on the property.
Our vision for the elevator is to create a new dining experience that cultivates
entrepreneurial activity and is fully rooted in the Cheyenne community. Our flavorful
offerings will include local fare from rotating food trucks, an innovative beverage
program, communal seating, private space for small events, and a craft liquor store.
We believe the blending of these individual project elements makes this application
particularly special and will serve as the catalyst the West Edge needs to sustain
momentum in transforming this emerging neighborhood hotspot.
Our team has carefully cross-referenced our vision with the liquor license options
available in Wyoming. While minimal elements of our vision might be possible under
another license, the cohesive packaging of this exciting concept would not progress
without your favorable consideration of our application for a Laramie County Retail

BACKGROUND
The Cheyenne Mill redevelopment project is located at W. 21st Street and Reed Avenue and is
part of the growing Reed Avenue Corridor revitalization effort. The Cheyenne Urban Renewal
Authority has identified the corridor as a redevelopment priority and has indicated plans to
establish a project area plan to support ongoing investment.
The developers are proposing an entertainment center focused around an 1,148 square foot
bar and a 561 square foot specialty liquor store. The project will also include outdoor patio
space to the east of the building and a 640 square foot party room for special events. Making
this project unique is the addition of up to two indoor staging areas that will be made
available for local food trucks. An additional game lawn and courtyard will be added to the
west of the building providing opportunities for entertainment and an additional food truck
landing pad.
Food trucks have become commonplace in bars and craft liquor establishments as they allow
for a rotating variety of food types. Often food trucks develop a community following, and
customers travel to their locations to enjoy their food. The developers have also indicated a
desire to focus on local food truck operators to support the local economy.

ECONOMIC IMPACT
To understand the impact, it is necessary to take a deeper dive into each of the project
elements.
LIQUOR STORE
For a liquor store to be successful the following elements are critical, (1) location, (2)
competition, (3) product mix, and (4) personalized customer service. The store will also need
to maintain a strong inventory and maximize the available space. The store will also have
collaborations with craft beer and liquor in the bar to promote sales. Within the area, there is
one convenience liquor store and lounge located on Lincolnway to the west. In addition, there
is a specialty liquor store on Carey Avenue, which provides the closest comparison for size and
product type.
Given the size and location of the liquor store, the estimate of total sales would range from
$375/square foot to $550/square foot. For purposes of this analysis, we used $550 /square
foot in part due to anticipated higher price points for premium beer and liquor.
BAR, EVENT SPACE
The bar and party room combined are just under 1,800 square feet. Given the uniqueness of
the space and the potential for redevelopment of the corridor the space would likely perform
at around $550 square foot with the potential to be much higher as the corridor continues to
improve. The patio space also adds additional room and an amenity during the warmer
summer months. The analysis includes additional sales from that patio discounted to 40
percent of the estimated indoor sales to reflect that patio activity will be most robust during
the warmer summer months. Howeve this activity could increase as well as the site
becomes more popular. Winter activities and festivals with propane heaters and firepit
amenities could extend the viable season in cooler weather months.
FOOD TRUCKS
In estimating the impact of the food trucks, it is important to recognize that the space would
allow for pull-in spaces to increase opportunities in inclement months. A rough estimate
would suggest that the bar would average seven to eight truck visits per week. This suggests
two on Fridays and Saturdays (4), plus Sunday afternoons and one or two additional days
during the week, which is estimated at around 390 food truck visits per year. Sales per evening
vary based on the time of year, and day or the week, but we would estimate for a truck to be
profitable, they would need to do around $1,500 in sales per night on average. For purposes
of the analysis, we would increase this by 25 percent to reflect the opportunity for take out
food from area residents and other customers.
