Strategies for Invigorating Downtown Cheyenne
Greetings!
Nearly every day people who care about our community take me aside to talk about downtown Cheyenne
and how to make it better.  “Better” means different things to different people, but these conversations
reveal common threads expressed by residents from all walks of life.  Some wax nostalgic for the
downtown Cheyenne they remember from childhood while others draw inspiration from travels to other
cities.   It’s clear that Cheyenne’s people yearn for a downtown that bustles with economic, cultural and
social activity:  a pleasant place to live, work, shop and dine as well as the capital city Wyoming deserves.
I share this vision for downtown Cheyenne.  Rather than dwell on the past, disparage our community or
bemoan the challenges inherent in reshaping our central business district, it is up to all of us to focus on
how to be the best Cheyenne we can be… and we’ve got so much to work with!  With its walkable scale,
striking historic buildings and established organizations, we already boast assets other communities crave.
Evidence suggests that downtown is on the rebound, from significant activity by developers and other
investors to new cultural organizations breathing life into once forlorn storefronts.  Every month new
shops and restaurants open their doors and people who want to live in an urban setting have more and
more options.  Downtown property owners approved a self-imposed millage to support the Downtown
Development Authority’s endeavors, voting with their pocketbooks.  Meanwhile Visit Cheyenne, Cheyenne
Frontier Days and numerous other organizations host events around Depot Plaza that introduce
newcomers to all that downtown offers and ensure that they enjoy spending time in Cheyenne… and
return with their friends.
Downtown’s charms notwithstanding, our greatest asset remains the talented people who live and work in
Cheyenne and are willing to band together on behalf of our community.  The Downtown Historic
Placemaking project drew diverse ideas and insights that reflect Cheyennites’ affinity for straight talk and
flair for approaching tough tasks with practical solutions and can-do spirit.  Tapping that energy is one
reason I look forward to partnering with Cheyenne citizens on downtown matters:  to get more strong
shoulders pushing the wheel.
In many ways, this document could be fairly described as a “Things to Do” list.  That straight-forward
approach is intentional, but the list itself reflects the hopes and frustrations of the 400+ people that took
part in the Historic Placemaking project.  We need to enlist and involve the numerous players that affect
downtown and can, working together, make it the lively place that belongs to everyone in our community.
Consequently, this plan responds to many diverse perspectives and includes implementation ideas geared
towards alternative points of view.  Some strategies you’ll read in this document describe long-term, large-
scale initiatives with the potential to, eventually, prompt the need to revise the very maps describing
downtown.  Others are as simple as sprucing up the building and streets.  All of them can use your help.
Join us!

Introduction
Across the country and around the world, certain
cities, both large and small, inspire deep affection
while others leave little lasting impression.  But
when people describe why they love a particular
city, they tend to cite a few specific features
followed by intangible aspects of their
experience, from a feeling of community to
friendly encounters with strangers to a
heightened sense of well-being.
In short, some cities exude vitality, foster
connections and promote possibilities and others
do not.  These places almost seem to exert a
positive influence on its people – and vice versa –
but how?
For decades urbanologists of all stripes have tried
to understand this dynamic.  It turns out that
physical and economic characteristics contribute
to:
•
Strong links to the past, which stirs a sense
of continuity and belonging;
•
Potential for a bright future, which fuels
optimism and the desire to invest time,
money and ideas;
•
Distinctive built environment with the
personality and scale that evokes positive
psychological responses.
Together, these facets of the urban experience
create a SENSE OF PLACE that grows stronger over
time.  While newcomers will never understand a
community’s sense of place as deeply as life-long
residents, even leisure travelers can recognize it
within a very short time.  PLACEMAKING
encompasses the process of identifying and
implementing strategies to reinforce the physical,
economic and social dimensions of experiencing
a city or a neighborhood.  Placemaking is
therefore the work of many professions,
including historic preservation, economic
development, design and others concerned with
aspects of community livability and quality of life.

FOUNDING CHEYENNE: AN OVERVIEW
In July of 1867, General Grenville M. Dodge,
a former Union General trained as a civil
engineer, arrived at the site that would
become Cheyenne, Wyoming.  After the
Civil War, Dodge remained with the U.S.
military and embarked on a new
assignment:  protecting the Bozeman Trail,
an overland mail route that connected
Montana’s gold rush territory with the
Oregon Trail.
Skirmishes with local Indians who had long
used the route ensued.  While escaping
from a war party, Dodge realized that he
had discovered a pass through the Laramie
Mountains that could serve the Union
Pacific Railroad well.  He resigned his
commission and went to work laying out
the Transcontinental Railway as the Union
Pacific’s chief engineer.
Dodge chose Cheyenne – the name would
come later – as the spot where the Union
Pacific Railroad would cross Crow Creek, a
South Platte River tributary.  He and his
survey crew laid out the site in one day, but
by the time the first rails were laid four
months later, over 4,000 newcomers had
already arrived, certain that the area would
bring them prosperity.  Many followed the
railroad further west, but many stayed and
a town was born.
About six months later, the U.S. Congress
established the Wyoming Territory, with
statehood following in 1890.  As the new
state’s most bustling metropolis, Cheyenne
became Wyoming’s capital city.

Executive Summary
Downtown Cheyenne presents a conundrum.  It
offers so many of the ingredients shared by
communities that radiate a strong sense of place,
from its historic structures and walkable streets
to its strong employment base as home to
Wyoming’s capital.  Several property owners are
investing in downtown Cheyenne and report
strong interest, especially as the region’s
economy rises in tandem with world-wide energy
demand.  And yet, vacant storefronts and
struggling businesses affirm the conventional
wisdom that downtown Cheyenne has yet to
meet its potential.
Over the years, Cheyenne’s leaders have invested
in downtown, recognizing that a vibrant central
business district nurtures the arts, small
businesses, property values and provides an
incubator for young companies getting a toehold
on profitability while reducing sprawl and its
costs.  The community – especially downtown
property owners – deserves immense credit for
establishing the Downtown Development
Authority, Cheyenne’s lead organization for
enhancing the CBD.
This report sets forth recommendations
stemming from a year-long collaboration
between the City, the DDA and over 400 of
downtown’s champions and stakeholders to
identify strategies to accelerate its recovery. The
Historic Placemaking Project emerged from
growing interest among community leaders in
exploring how downtown Cheyenne can
progress, adapting and evolving to fulfill the
regions’ needs today and tomorrow.
PLACEMAKING refers to both the process and the
activities that together create enjoyable,
distinctive communities where authentic
environments meet the needs of residents,
employers and tourists.  The Downtown Historic
Placemaking Project strived to support
partnerships and implementation from the start,
involving hundreds of stakeholders in the quest
for effective strategic initiatives.  That meant
confronting dysfunctional aspects of the system
for fostering change downtown.  Frustration with
most of the organizations working downtown
abounded, making normal communications
hitches even more salient.  Finally, the dialogue
between the private sector and downtown’s
stewards reflects years of both good will and
exasperation, making for productive partnerships
punctuated by occasional mutual aggravation.
Fortunately, resources for addressing
downtown’s unmet potential include:  committed
leaders, attentive stakeholders; a full
complement of development organizations;
definitive legislative authority; and the financial
wherewithal to make a difference.
During the Downtown Historic Placemaking
process, stakeholders suggested hundreds of
ideas for how to deploy these resources in the
service of downtown.  These ideas were
consolidated into 40 strategic initiatives
organized in two ways:  first, by the logical
organization with primary implementation
responsibility, and; second, using seven “bucket”
issue/action categories describing downtown’
physical plant, business life and future
development prospects.  The strategies are
further distinguished by ease of implementation
and impact along with specific steps, budgets,
success metrics, and timelines.
The strategies cover about five years of activity
and represent investments totaling about $5.4
million.  As inter-related and overlapping factors,
all inflict reciprocal effects on downtown
Cheyenne.  The chart that follows summarizes
the strategies.  It’s important to note that the
timeline and budget reflect the initial period of
intense activity and investment of both human
and financial resources.  However, many of these
initiatives represent ongoing activities that will
require continued and persistent work, including
periodic assessments and course corrections.
Together, the strategies create an action agenda
that requires strong leadership, bold vision and
persistent effort.  There is plenty to do – not just
for this region’s community, tourism and
economic development interests and
government agencies.  The strategies outline
significant roles – both as leaders and as partners
– for Cheyenne’s arts organizations, educational
institutions, property owners, lenders, and more.
In short, everyone has a stake in downtown
Cheyenne and can help shape its future.
Downtowns succeed when they are true to their
communities; implementing these strategies
needs to occur while putting Cheyenne’s interest
and character first.  The result:  a downtown
Cheyenne that pulses with rhythmic activity, the
beating of Wyoming’s and Cheyenne’s emotional
and economic heart.

About the Historic Placemaking Project
Across the country, stewards of vibrant
downtowns point to historic building stock as a
key ingredient in creating places with character.
In Cheyenne, however, many of downtown’s
historic structures remain vacant or
underutilized… their potential unfulfilled.
Frustrated public and private sector leaders
questioned why economic growth in southeast
Wyoming failed to stimulate significant
investment downtown.  Exasperated citizens,
business people and property owners vented
about downtown’s woes to elected officials, the
newspaper and each other.
The Downtown Historic Placemaking Project
emerged from the growing conviction among
community leaders that an outside perspective
was needed to uncover the root of the problem.
Was there something inherently wrong with
downtown’s building stock, like small floor plates
or inadequate parking?  Were the various
organizations charged with caring for downtown
too feeble – not enough resources, perhaps – to
do the job?  Or did it have to do with City politics
and priorities?  Was it the national economic
malaise?  Could it be Cheyenne’s image as a
rustic frontier town that gave lenders and
developers from out of state pause?
The theories were many and varied but shared an
underlying lament:  given all that Cheyenne has
going for it, why does downtown struggle when
other communities with fewer intrinsic
advantages attract investment and prosper?
What is Placemaking?
PLACEMAKING refers to both the process and the
activities that together create enjoyable,
distinctive communities where authentic
environments meet the needs of residents,
employers and tourists.  PLACEMAKING emphasizes
responding to community concerns and creating
partnerships for change over the impulse to lay
out plans and propose design solutions.
PLACEMAKING acknowledges that the cumulative
impact of simple efforts can be as effective – and
certainly faster and cheaper – than implementing
large scale development projects, reasoning that
the former will strengthen the market the latter
requires.  Respectful of market realities and the
transformative effect big projects can have,
PLACEMAKING also believes that individuals can
make a big difference to downtowns, recruiting
independent thinkers, entrepreneurs and urban
pioneers to the table.  Similarly, PLACEMAKING
works from the principle that the quirky aspects
of community lend charm and distinctiveness.
They should be nurtured and celebrated to
inspire devotion among downtown’s numerous
constituencies, rather than tamped down in the
name of progress.  Last, but not least,
PLACEMAKING sees community building as an
ongoing process that never really ends; change is
inevitable, so PLACEMAKING must abide in
perpetuity.
On the Process
The Downtown Historic Placemaking Project
strived for a process that supported partnerships
and implementation from the start.  That means:
•
Consulting with stakeholders in meaningful
ways, listening to their concerns and ideas
and incorporating insights into the work
during 107 private meetings and a series of
eight public community forums;
•
Maintaining momentum and enthusiasm;
•
Treating Placemaking as a system and
recognizing that all strategies emerging from
the work will be intertwined, in keeping with
how development and redevelopment
decisions reflect the interplay between
numerous factors encompassing market,
regulatory, finance, risk tolerance and other
characteristics unique to opportunities in
downtown Cheyenne today;
•
Ensuring that participants understand other
points of view;
•
Seeking to get some projects off the ground
as soon as possible;
•
Using available resources efficiently.
The process succeeded in many regards, although
the goal of getting projects started proved
elusive.  Initial optimism gave way to a deeper
appreciation of the barriers – economic and
organizational – that stymie progress in
downtown Cheyenne.

Frustration and Hope in
Downtown Cheyenne
Asking about downtown Cheyenne elicits
different responses.  Mood swings are more the
norm:  pessimism followed swiftly by assertions
of affection for Downtown Cheyenne’s special
people and places.
In gloomy or cynical moments, people pointed to
lack of progress as evidenced by empty
storefronts, vacant buildings, deferred
maintenance, absentee owners, litter, snow piles,
uneven parking enforcement, cluttered alleys,
dirty windows, panhandlers and organizations
they deemed wasteful, indifferent or both.  In
sunnier frames of mind, people took pride in
successful past and present redevelopment
projects, described their favorite shops,
recommended restaurants, urged attendance at
area museums, performances and presentations,
pointed out examples of architectural excellence,
and shared happy memories of concerts, parades
and festivals over the years.
The sense that Cheyenne is poised on the
precipice of change is striking.  “We’re on the
cusp,” people agreed, but yet no clear consensus
on what should emerge next.
What’s Working Downtown
and What Isn’t?
Downtown Cheyenne benefits from a series of
assets with potential to accelerate its re-
invigoration, including:
•
The lovely restoration of the historic Depot,
the creation of adjacent Depot Plaza and the
varied and interesting programming it
supports;
•
Successful private sector investments in
activity-generating uses, like the Plains
Hotel, Suite 1901 and the Boyd Building;
•
New rehabilitation investments in historic
structures, such as the former Dinneen
showroom and Grier furniture building;
•
Thriving retail businesses including the
Lincolnway “Women’s Block” cluster of
home décor and fashion stores and other
long-term downtown denizens offering
specialty goods, like Wyoming Home and
Bohemian Metals;
•
Salons, spas and personal care emporia, like ,
Adora, Epitome, 17 Street Spa, and Noire
Cocktail Salon.
•
Cultural amenities, from venerable

Little Theater to creative upstarts like Lights

several galleries offering fine arts, like

creative people downtown;
•
Museums that reinforce Cheyenne’s
Western Heritage, including the Nelson
Museum of the West, Cowgirls of the West
and the Depot Museum;
•
Lodging accommodations to suit different
tastes and budgets, including the Plains
Hotel and Nagle Warren Mansion;
•
Steady sources of daytime market support
from approximately 16,400 employees
working in downtown for the Cheyenne
Regional Medical Center and federal, state
and local governments;
•
Walkability features including short blocks,
traffic calming improvements, wide
sidewalks, crosswalks and street furniture;
•
Affordable commercial real estate which has
made it easier for new enterprises to get
started and established ones to prosper.
New restaurants opening their doors within
the past several years include El Charrito, the
Morris House Bistro, Paramount Café,
Wipeout Coffee, Mitchell’s BBQ, Re-O-Na
Sushi and Thai Restaurant and Anong’s Thai
Cuisine.  The cluster along 17th Street –
Rubyjuice, Sanford’s, Two Doors Down,
