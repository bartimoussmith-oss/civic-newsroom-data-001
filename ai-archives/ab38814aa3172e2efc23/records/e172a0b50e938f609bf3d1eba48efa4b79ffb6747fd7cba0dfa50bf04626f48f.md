MA-25 Public and Assisted Housing – 91.210(b)
Introduction
The Cheyenne Housing Authority (CHA) owns and manages a variety of properties in Cheyenne and
Laramie, including single-family homes and apartments. In Cheyenne, the CHA also offers apartment
communities specifically designed to serve elderly and disabled individuals. All CHA Public Housing
properties in Cheyenne and Laramie are smoke-free, ensuring a healthier environment for residents.
These housing units are subsidized by the U.S. Department of Housing and Urban Development (HUD)
and are maintained in full compliance with HUD regulations. The CHA continually accepts applications
for its programs, including the Housing Choice Voucher Program—commonly known as Section 8. This
federal program is the primary means by which very low-income households, as well as elderly and
disabled persons, receive assistance to afford decent, safe, and sanitary housing in the private market
and Qualified households participating in the Housing Choice Voucher Program receive vouchers to help
subsidize their rent payments to landlords of privately owned properties. Participants are responsible
for finding a suitable housing unit of their choice, provided the landlord agrees to participate in the
program and the unit meets the CHA’s minimum health and safety standards. The CHA pays a housing
subsidy directly to the landlord on behalf of the participating household, while the household pays the
difference between the actual rent and the subsidized amount.
Of the 316 residents occupying the 316 units that the CHA reported in use as of February 2025, 100% of
those households requested accessibility features that were current residents of those units, 192
reported being elderly or disabled. Waitlists are long and they have a limited number of accessible units.

Describe the restoration and revitalization needs of public housing units in the
jurisdiction:
The CHA’s public housing units require ongoing restoration and revitalization to remain safe, decent,
and affordable for residents. The primary needs include capital improvements such as replacing roofs,
windows, siding, and major building systems, as well as upgrades to flooring, sidewalks, and heating.
Regular maintenance and prompt repairs are essential to address tenant requests, prevent
deterioration, and ensure units meet safety and quality standards.
A significant challenge is compliance with federal requirements, especially regarding lead-based paint
hazards in older units. Cheyenne faces a shortage of certified inspectors and abatement professionals,
which can slow down necessary rehabilitation and limit the number of units that can be improved each
year. All restoration efforts must also bring units up to City and HUD minimum housing standards,
ensuring structural integrity, weather-tightness, and the removal of hazardous materials.

MA-40 Barriers to Affordable Housing – 91.210(e)
Describe any negative effects of public policies on affordable housing and
residential investment.
Wyoming faces a critical shortage of nonprofit developers and limited funding for affordable housing.
The state must add tens of thousands of new homes by 2030 to meet projected demand, but the
current shortfall is especially severe for extremely low-income households, who have far fewer
affordable and available rental homes. This disproportionately impacts extremely low-income residents.
According to the National Low Income Housing Coalition 2025 Wyoming Housing Profile- only 52 rental
homes are affordable and available for every 100 extremely low-income household and 72% of
extremely low-income renters are severely cost-burdened. Through consultation with affordable
housing developers and as part of the updated 2025 Analysis to Impediments to Fair Housing Study,
Wyoming’s affordable housing sector has identified several significant barriers that hinder the
development of new affordable homes, particularly for extremely low-income households.
Wyoming lacks both a State Revolving Loan Fund and a State Housing Trust Fund, making it one of only a
few states without such resources. A recent legislative effort to create a State Housing Trust Fund with
substantial annual funding did not advance. This fund would have supported new construction,
rehabilitation, maintenance, and homelessness initiatives, and included a revolving loan component at
or below market rates, as well as capacity-building for affordable housing development.
Six bills were introduced during the 2025 session to simplify housing development by removing barriers
or establishing funding, but none passed. For example, House Bill 68 (HB0068), which would have
supported tax increment financing for affordable housing by expanding the Wyoming Urban Renewal
Code, died in committee. Another attempt to amend the Zoning Protest Petition law—which allows a
small percentage of nearby property owners to force a supermajority city council vote on
developments—also failed. This law recently blocked a large apartment project and left the site vacant
during a period of high demand.
Developers struggle to make projects financially feasible, particularly due to infrastructure costs.
Wyoming’s infrastructure funding, managed by the State Lands and Investment Board (SLIB), is
accessible only to municipalities, not private developers or nonprofits. Wyoming’s state constitution
restricts funding to nonprofits except for direct aid to the poor, which does not include affordable
housing development. As a result, when municipalities need to apply for the funds, projects are subject
to public processes where resident opposition can halt developments. Nonprofits and developers may
apply for federal funding through state-administered programs, but these resources are limited, highly
competitive.

MA-45 Non-Housing Community Development Assets – 91.215 (f)
Introduction
Cheyenne’s location at the intersection of I-80 and I-25 in southeastern Wyoming position the City as a
regional center for employment and business activity. The city has seen an increase in new businesses
and development, resulting in a range of job opportunities. According to recent data, the largest
business sectors are education and health, finance, insurance and real estate, and arts, entertainment,
and accommodations-including gambling facilities. Together, these sectors account for 43% of jobs in
Cheyenne, and new venues such as Horse Palace Swan Ranch are projected to add more than 140 jobs.
Job growth is expected in these sectors as well as in construction, indicating a continued need for skilled
workers. Efforts to support job training programs, job placement services, and infrastructure
improvements are likely to play a role in meeting workforce demands and maintaining economic
stability. These initiatives may help workers develop the skills needed for available jobs, but challenges
remain in ensuring that growth is balanced and benefits are widely shared across the community.

ensuring that every resident and business in Wyoming has access to affordable, reliable, redundant, and
future-proof broadband service. The program currently includes over fifty broadband providers
statewide, all of which participate in the Affordability Connectivity Program. Through these efforts, the
Broadband Program is actively working to improve both the connectivity and affordability of broadband
for communities across Wyoming (WCDA 2023-2027 Con Plan).

99.78% of the city. The average home in Cheyenne can access speeds up to 2,270 Mbps, with residents
able to choose from between 1 and 10 providers depending on their location. Service types available
include fixed wireless, DSL, cable, and fiber, though availability may vary by neighborhood. This level of
provider diversity has led to a generally healthy competitive environment.

SP-55 Barriers to affordable housing – 91.215(h)
Barriers to Affordable Housing
Wyoming faces a critical shortage of nonprofit developers and limited funding for affordable housing.
The state must add tens of thousands of new homes by 2030 to meet projected demand, but the
current shortfall is especially severe for extremely low-income households, who have far fewer
affordable and available rental homes. This disproportionately impacts extremely low-income residents.
According to the National Low Income Housing Coalition 2025 Wyoming Housing Profile- only 52 rental
homes are affordable and available for every 100 extremely low-income households and 72% of
extremely low-income renters are severely cost-burdened. Through consultation with affordable
housing developers and as part of the updated 2025 Analysis to Impediments to Fair Housing Study,
Wyoming’s affordable housing sector has identified several significant barriers that hinder the
development of new affordable homes, particularly for extremely low-income households.
Wyoming lacks both a State Revolving Loan Fund and a State Housing Trust Fund, making it one of only a
few states without such resources. A recent legislative effort to create a Wyoming State Housing Trust
Fund with substantial annual funding did not advance. This fund would have supported new
construction, rehabilitation, maintenance, and homelessness initiatives, and included a revolving loan
component at or below market rates, as well as capacity-building for affordable housing development.
Six bills were introduced during the 2025 session to simplify housing development by removing barriers
or establishing funding, but none passed. For example, House Bill 68 (HB0068), which would have
supported tax increment financing for affordable housing by expanding the Wyoming Urban Renewal
Code, died in committee. Another attempt to amend the Zoning Protest Petition law—which allows a
small percentage of nearby property owners to force a supermajority city council vote on
developments—also failed. This law recently blocked a large apartment project in Cheyenne and left the
site vacant during a period of high demand.

Developers struggle to make projects financially feasible, particularly due to infrastructure costs.

Introduction
The City has allocated funds for the Smith Manor Transitional Housing Sidewalk Repair and Replacement
project, which will upgrade transitional housing facilities and benefit approximately 18 individuals each
year.
During the previous Con Planning period, CDBG funds were allocated for home rehabilitation.
Rehabilitation is not included as a priority need in the 2025-2027 Con Plan as the subrecipient
experienced challenges coordinating the repairs projects, particularly, issues meeting extensive
environmental review requirements. The funds were recently returned and will be used to fund facility
rehabilitation or infrastructure improvements that benefit low- to moderate- income households.
Other affordable housing activities, such as the acquisition of existing units, were also excluded from the
upcoming plan due to difficulties in spending previously allocated funds, largely because of complex
environmental review requirements and limited affordable housing inventory. The City is reassessing
which activities can be implemented more efficiently and is seeking ways to better align projects with
regulatory requirements and local organizational capacity. Additionally, the City will begin working with
consulting agencies to conduct CEST-level and EA-level environmental reviews for larger projects. This
approach will relieve staff of this responsibility and allow experts with specialized knowledge of
environmental regulations to complete the work.

Rental assistance remains an important priority. While applications for the 2025 program year were
submitted by nonprofits to provide rental assistance, the H&CD Advisory Council prioritized other public
service needs due to high demand, organizational capacity, and past performance considerations. The
City remains committed to partnering with strong organizations and encourages future project
proposals that provide public facility or infrastructure improvements for LMI households, ensuring CDBG
resources continue to support Cheyenne’s affordable housing and community development goals.
