Cheyenne/Laramie County Emergency Management
Emergency Operations Plan Summary
Albin, Burns, Cheyenne, Pine Bluffs

This plan is the revision of what was previously titled “Emergency Response Plan”.  Necessary
changes were put in the plan so EMA would be more in line with State Statutes and the National
Response Framework.  The new integrated Emergency Operations Plan for Laramie County and
the cities/towns therein as a City - County Program.  This plan supersedes any previous
emergency management/civil defense plans promulgated by the county for this purpose.  It
provides a frame-work in which the departments of each city, town and the county can plan and
perform their respective emergency functions during a disaster or national emergency.
This plan recognizes the need of ongoing Emergency Management Planning and Coordination
by all jurisdictions of government within Laramie County.  This plan attempts to be all inclusive in
combining the phases of Emergency Management, which are: (1) Mitigation, (2) Preparedness,
(3) Response, (4) Recovery, and (5) Prevention.

In summary, the plan covers the following:
Base Plan
Introduction to the plan
Vulnerabilities
Roles and Responsibilities for Senior Officials
Emergency Operations Center Operations – leadership & activation protocols
Public Information & Warning
Emergency and Disaster Assistance – State and Federal process

Emergency Support Functions (ESF)
This section defines and describes the coordination for the 15 ESF’s (The Means), Lifelines (The
Ends) and Core Capabilities (The Ways) in which we coordinate with all community partners
during all levels of EOC activation for emergency response, Planning, and Training needs.

Incident Support Annex
The Incident Annex identifies and outlines specific incidents that have the highest probability of
occurring within Laramie County.   Each annex provides a description of each event, the effects
it could potentially have within the county and an outline of roles, responsibilities, and coordinating
efforts for each type of event.
Support Annexes
The Support Annex Assistance Guide outlines specific tasks or functions that may be carried out
during and after an emergency or a disaster. The guide describes, in specific detail, the
operational system identified in the Cheyenne/Laramie County Emergency Operations Plan that
will be used to carry out the functions listed.
Planning Annexes
The Planning Annex identifies specific plans within the ERP that may be utilized and followed
during and after an emergency or a disaster. These annexes are designed to be the major support
system for the EOC. These planning annexes play an important part in the needs of the affected
jurisdiction to provide logistics, coordination, documentation, and help provide additional support
to the incident.

Introduction
The Cheyenne/Laramie County Emergency Management Agency takes a proactive role in
potential disasters and emergencies, either natural or manmade, for the purposes of
“Preparedness, Response, Recovery, Mitigation and Protection” of Laramie County and its
citizens. This plan is designed to lessen, or in some cases, prevent loss of life and property during
disaster situations.
The goal of the Cheyenne/Laramie County Emergency Operations Plan is to bring together a
strong team of agency representatives whose early involvement in an event will expedite the
coordination of local resources to assist an impacted area. With the assistance of this plan, the
team coordinates the efforts of local services to restore, supply or re-supply local infrastructure
and needs of the communities within Laramie County.
The Cheyenne/Laramie County Emergency Management Agency (CLCEMA) is tasked under
Wyoming Statute § 19-13-101 et seq. for developing and implementing a crisis operations plan
for coordinating local response to events.  The Emergency Management Agency along with local
first responders and emergency support functions have built this plan to assign responsibilities
for carrying out essential support functions and event specific actions.
This plan is updated to ensure compliance with both the “National Incident Management System”
(NIMS) and the “National Response Framework” (NRF), but more importantly the “Wyoming
Response Plan” (WRP). The State of Wyoming will become our closest ally in times of crisis. They
will provide a network of availability to both state level resources and assists in federal response
when applicable.
This plan consists of a “Base Plan”, “Emergency Support Functions” (ESFs), “Incident Specific
Annexes”, “Support Annexes”, and “Planning Annexes”. The Base Plan provides the initial
“framework” for the government’s response to incident management.  It follows “the Incident
Command System” (ICS), allowing for expansion and contraction of the organization depending
on the needs of the responders and complexity of the incident. The ESFs (listed later in the plan)
are used to support the ICS and provide specific needs during an incident. The Incident Specific
Annexes are detailed to a pre-determined event, being natural or manmade.
As a representative of government (elected or employed) or as a member of emergency
services, it is imperative you know the content of the plan. You can and will play an
important role in the government’s response to a large scale emergency or disaster.  Your
preparedness is an essential part of the successful implementation and outcome of this
plan by providing the citizens with both “Continuity of Operations” and “Continuity of
Government”.

Thank you,
Cheyenne/Laramie County Emergency Management Agency

Purpose
The purpose of the Cheyenne/Laramie County Emergency Operations Plan (EOP) is to establish
a comprehensive, local, all-hazards approach to emergency incident coordination and
management across a spectrum of activities including prevention, preparedness, response,
recovery and protection.
The EOP incorporates best practices and procedures from various incident management
disciplines—homeland security, emergency management, law enforcement, firefighting,
hazardous materials response, public works, public health, emergency medical services, and
responder and recovery worker health and safety—and integrates them into a unified coordinating
structure.
The EOP provides the framework for local interaction with other local governments, state, and
federal governments; the private sector; and other non-governmental organizations in the context
of domestic incident prevention, preparedness, response, and recovery activities. It describes
capabilities and resources and establishes responsibilities, operational processes, and protocols
to help protect Laramie County from natural and manmade hazards including terrorism; save
lives; protect public health, safety, property, and the environment; and reduce adverse
psychological consequences and disruptions. Finally, the EOP serves as the foundation for the
development of detailed annexes and procedures to effectively and efficiently implement local
incident management activities and assistance in the context of specific types of incidents.
The EOP, using NIMS, establishes mechanisms to maximize the integration of incident-related
prevention, preparedness, response, and recovery activities. The EOP will improve coordination
and integration of federal, state, local, regional, private-sector, and non-governmental
organization partners. It also maximizes efficient utilization of resources needed for effective
incident management and critical infrastructure/key resources protection and restoration.
Scope
The EOP covers the full range of complex and constantly changing requirements in anticipation
of or in response to natural and man-made disasters, emergencies, threats or acts of terrorism.
The EOP also provides the basis to initiate long-term community recovery and mitigation
activities. The EOP establishes interagency and multi-jurisdictional mechanisms for local
government involvement in, and coordination of, emergency incident management operations.
This plan distinguishes between incidents that require only local coordination vs. incidents that
require assistance from state and federal partners.
This plan is applicable to all local departments and agencies that may be requested to provide
assistance or conduct operations in the context of actual or potential local emergency incidents.
Coordination and response within this plan is in line with the interrelationships of the Emergency
Support Functions (ESF), Core Capabilities, and Community Lifelines as identified within the
National Response Framework. The EOP is applicable to incidents that may occur at sites under
the control of Laramie County government and/or any jurisdiction within Laramie County.

Emergency Operations Plan Applicability
The EOP applies to all local departments and agencies that may be requested to provide
assistance or conduct operations in actual or potential local emergency incidents. These incidents
require a coordinated response by an appropriate combination of Federal, State, local, private-
sector, and nongovernmental entities.
Situation & Assumptions
Situation
Laramie County sits on a plateau in the southeast corner of Wyoming, covering an area of 2685
square miles. Elevation varies from 7000 feet at the western boundary to 5000 feet at the eastern
boundary (Cheyenne-6082 ft.; Pine Bluffs-5042 ft.). The area is mostly “High Plains” grassland
with a total of 8.3 square miles of surface water.
Laramie County has a total population of 100,723 (2022 Census Data) with a labor force of an
estimated 49,175. Most lands are privately owned with agriculture as the primary use. The
assessed valuation is $1.5 Billion with total taxes levied of $102 Million.
Average per capita income is an estimated $341,104. With three levels of government in the
county, the majority of employment is government or government related.
Laramie County’s demographics are quickly changing as Cheyenne and the surrounding county
grows. Light industrial areas are growing, bringing in larger businesses. These businesses bring
employment making residential subdivisions and business parks grow, causing both the city and
county population to expand.
Cheyenne is a regional transportation hub. Cheyenne is a main hub for the Union Pacific Railroad
(UPRR) that still uses the same east-west route laid out in the 1860’s. Cheyenne sits at the
intersection of two major interstates, I-25 and I-80, which results in large amounts of truck traffic
flow through the county.
The Federal Government plays a major roll for Cheyenne and Laramie County. Located adjacent
to Cheyenne is F.E. Warren Air Force Base, a nuclear missile facility that maintains and operates
the Minuteman missile sites located throughout the region. The Federal Courts, U.S. Marshall’s
Office, Bureau of Land Management and Internal Revenue Service all have offices located in
Cheyenne.

Laramie County does not provide water or sewer infrastructure for most residential areas with the
exception of the South Cheyenne Water and Sewer District. Water for this district is purchased
from the Cheyenne Board of Public Utilities. Most county residences use private well and septic
systems.
County electric utilities are provided by local grids managed by a private utility company and a
Utility Coop in the eastern portion of the county. The smaller communities of eastern Laramie
County have limited utility infrastructure using well water systems and either waste collection
ponds or private septic systems.

Cheyenne’s infrastructure is multi-faceted. Cheyenne’s electricity is supplied mostly from the
south out of Colorado supported by a redundant grid system. Water is supplied by pipeline from
the mountains west of Laramie, Wyoming to a new water treatment plant and supplemented by
numerous wells. Natural gas is supplied from several pipelines to a local distribution system.
Sewer treatment facilities located east of town are secured and controlled, as is the water
treatment plant.
Emergency services in the City of Cheyenne and Laramie County are provided by numerous fire
protection districts and municipal fire services, government law enforcement and both contracted
and volunteer EMS providers. F.E. Warren AFB and the Wyoming Air National Guard also provide
full time fire and airport protection services. Several industrial units, such as Holly Frontier Oil
Refinery and Dyno Nobel give specific assistance to the emergency responders through mutual
aid agreements. Both state and federal law enforcement agencies can, and do, assist locals when
needed.
Cheyenne and Laramie County has experienced two large scale disasters requiring assistance
from both the State of Wyoming and the Federal Government. Both the tornado of 1979 and the
flash flood of 1985 proved to be deadly storms that demonstrated the need for this plan.
Assumptions
The EOP is predicated on a realistic approach to the problems likely to be encountered in Laramie
County during major emergencies and/or disasters, therefore:
1. Laramie County and its cities and towns will continue to be exposed to the hazards
identified in this plan as well as others which may develop in the future.
2. An emergency or disaster may occur at any time of the day or night, weekend or holiday
with little or no warning and may affect more than one jurisdiction.
3. Local government officials, both elected and appointed, will, to the best of their abilities
carry out all responsibilities regarding public safety and the protection of property.  These
responsibilities include attention to all phases of emergency management and provisions
to ensure the continuity of an effective, constitutional form of government.
4. Disasters may affect citizens in a larger geographical area near Laramie County.
Resources from all levels of government could be utilized to combat injury and death to a
larger area.
5. Incident management activities will be initiated and conducted using the principles of the
Incident Command System (ICS) and the National Incident Management System (NIMS).
6. Incidents could result in numerous injuries, fatalities, displaced victims, property loss,
disruption of normal life support systems, essential public services and basic infrastructure
and significant damage to the environment.
7. Incidents could overwhelm the capabilities of the local first responder network causing the
need for assistance from state and federal agencies.
8. Some citizens will ignore, not hear, or not understand warning of impending disaster
broadcast from media or local warning systems.
9. Although warning systems may be operational they may not be heard by all citizens.
10. Incidents are managed at the lowest possible geographic, organizational and jurisdictional
level.

Results and Methodology
Using existing hazards data, plans from participating jurisdictions, and input gained through
planning and public meetings, the HMPC agreed upon a list of hazards that could affect Laramie
County and/or the City of Cheyenne.  Hazards data from FEMA, the Wyoming Office of Homeland
Security (including the 2021-2026 Wyoming State Hazard Mitigation Plan), the National Oceanic
and Atmospheric Administration, the Spatial Hazard Events and Losses Database for the United
States (SHELDUS), and many other sources were examined to assess the significance of these
hazards to the planning area.  The hazards evaluated in this plan include those that have occurred
historically or have the potential to cause significant human and/or monetary losses in the future.
In 2016 the State of Wyoming determined Mitigation Planning throughout the state would become
regionalized and therefore combining all Region 7 hazard mitigation plans which include Laramie,
Goshen, and Platte Counties.  Within the regional plan each county’s hazards are individually
identified and identified as a whole for the jurisdiction.  Members of the HMPC used a hazards
worksheet to rate the significance of hazards that could potentially affect the participating
jurisdictions.  Significance was measured in general terms, focusing on key criteria such as the
likelihood of the event, past occurrences, spatial extent, and damage and casualty potential.  Note
that the significance of the hazard may vary from jurisdiction to jurisdiction.

Disaster Declaration History
As part of the hazard identification process, the HMPC researched past events that triggered
federal and/or state emergency or disaster declarations in the planning area.  Federal and/or state
disaster declarations may be granted when the severity and magnitude of an event surpasses the
ability of the local government to respond and recover.  Disaster assistance is supplemental and
sequential.  When the local government’s capacity has been surpassed, a state disaster
declaration may be issued, allowing for the provision of state assistance.  Should the disaster be
so severe that both the local and state governments’ capacities are exceeded; a federal
emergency or disaster declaration may be issued allowing for the provision of federal assistance.
The federal government may issue a disaster declaration through FEMA, the U.S. Department of
Agriculture (USDA), and/or the Small Business Administration (SBA).  FEMA also issues
emergency declarations, which are more limited in scope and without the long-term federal
recovery programs of major disaster declarations.  The quantity and types of damage are the
determining factors.
A USDA declaration will result in the implementation of the Emergency Loan Program through
the Farm Services Agency.  This program enables eligible farmers and ranchers in the affected
county as well as contiguous counties to apply for low interest loans.  A USDA declaration will
automatically follow a major disaster declaration for counties designated as major disaster areas
and those that are contiguous to declared counties, including those that are across state lines.
As part of an agreement with the USDA, the SBA offers low interest loans for eligible businesses
that suffer economic losses in declared and contiguous counties that have been declared by the
USDA.  These loans are referred to as Economic Injury Disaster Loans.
Roles and Responsibilities
A mayor, city representative, county commissioner or elected official, as a jurisdiction’s Chief
Elected Official (CEO), is responsible for the public safety and welfare of the people of that
jurisdiction. The local CEO is responsible for coordinating with Emergency Management and local
resources to address the full spectrum of actions to prevent, prepare for, respond to, and recover
from incidents involving all hazards including terrorism, natural disasters, accidents, and other
contingencies dependent upon State and local law. A CEO has extraordinary powers to suspend
local laws and ordinances, such as to establish a curfew, direct evacuations, and, in coordination
with the local health authority, to order a quarantine. Officials may provide leadership and play a
key role in communicating to the public, and in helping people, businesses, and organizations
cope with the consequences of any type of domestic incident within the jurisdiction. Officials also
negotiate and enter into mutual aid agreements with other jurisdictions to facilitate resource-
sharing; and requests State and, if necessary, Federal assistance through the Governor of the
State when the jurisdiction’s capabilities have been exceeded or exhausted.

This plan is based upon the concept that emergencies begin and end at the local level.  The
emergency support functions, performed by various groups responding to an emergency, will
coordinate together under the Lifelines concept to ensure planning and response efforts are being
met to the best of their ability.  All levels of emergency services will utilize both the “Incident

A comprehensive emergency operations plan is concerned with all types of hazards that may
develop in the community.  As shown below, it addresses a comprehensive approach to
emergency management including the 5 Phases of Emergency Management.
Mitigation:  Mitigation activities are those that eliminate or reduce the probability of a
disaster occurring. It also includes those long-term actions that lessen the undesirable
effects of unavoidable hazards.
Preparedness:  Preparedness actions serve to develop the response capabilities needed
in the event an emergency should arise.  Planning, training and exercises are among the
activities conducted under this phase.
Response:  Response is the actual providing of emergency services during a crisis.
These actions help to reduce casualties and damage, and speed recovery.  Response
activities include warning, evacuation, rescue, and similar operations.
Recovery:  Recovery is both a short-term and long-term process. Short-term operations
seek to restore vital services to the community and to provide the basic needs of the public.
Long-term recovery focuses on restoring the community to its normal, or improved, state
of affairs. Examples of recovery actions include restoration of non-vital government
services and reconstruction in damaged areas.  The recovery period offers an opportune
time to institute mitigation measures, particularly those related to the recent disaster.
Protection:  The capabilities necessary to secure the homeland against acts of terrorism
and manmade or natural disasters.

Joint Information System (JIS)
The JIS is a network of PIOs working together to deliver accurate, timely information the public
needs and wants. PIOs can use any variety of methods to talk or provide information with other
PIOs. The system should ensure clear, accurate information is being delivered amid the confusion
of disaster response. No one person is in charge of the JIS, it is a cooperative effort. The JIS
mirrors the ICS structure, so the primary responding agency will most likely take the lead in
releasing information. The JIS gives the PIOs the ability to share information, resources and
equipment designed to enhance response ability.
Joint Information Center (JIC)
The JIC is a central location to facilitate operation of the JIS during and after the event. The JIC
enhances information coordination, reduces misinformation and maximizes resources.  The JIC
is a physical location with tools to enhance the flow of public information. It provides a central
working facility where PIOs can gather. The JIC allows PIOs to handle increased information
needs by the media and the public. The JIC can provide “one-stop shopping” for the media to get
official information. A JIC can be as simple as a small office or the corner of a command center
or as complex as a pre-established facility with multiple phone lines, full media monitoring and
news conference stage.

Federal Assistance
As established in the Robert T. Stafford Disaster Relief and Emergency Assistance Act (Stafford
Act), all requests for a Declaration by the President that a major disaster exists shall be made by
the Governor. The Stafford Act includes two types of disaster declarations:

Emergency Declarations: The President can declare an emergency for any occasion or
instance when the President determines federal assistance is needed. Emergency
declarations supplement state and local efforts in providing emergency services.

Major Disaster Declarations: The President can declare a major disaster for any natural
event that the President determines has caused damage of such severity that it is beyond
the combined capabilities of state and local governments to respond. A major disaster
declaration provides a wide range of federal assistance programs for individuals and
public infrastructure, including funds for both emergency and permanent work.
Federal assistance through a Presidential Disaster Declaration is requested by the Governor if
the situation meets the criteria for a declaration. The Governor submits a written request to the
president through FEMA Region VIII in Denver, CO. FEMA gathers information to supplement the
Governor's request, and this is sent to the President who determines the final disposition. The
disposition of the request is transmitted through FEMA Region VIII back to the Governor. FEMA,
part of the Emergency Preparedness and Response Directorate of the Department of Homeland
Security, is tasked with coordinating the response of federal agencies with state and local
governments.
Based on the Governor's request, the President may declare that a Major Disaster or Emergency
exists, thus activating an array of federal programs to assist in the response and recovery effort.
Not all programs, however, are activated for every disaster. The determination of which programs
are activated is based on the needs found during the damage assessment and any subsequent
information that may be discovered.
FEMA disaster assistance falls into three general categories:

Individual Assistance — aid to individuals and households;

Public Assistance — aid to public (and certain private non-profit) entities for certain
emergency services and the repair or replacement of disaster-damaged public facilities;

Hazard Mitigation Assistance — funding for measures designed to reduce future losses
to public and private property.
Some declarations will provide only individual assistance or only public assistance. Hazard
mitigation opportunities are assessed in most situations. Procurement Disaster Assistance Teams
(PDAT) may be available as well to support in the disaster recovery process.  CLCEMA in
coordination with WOHS will provide specific rules, regulations, and templates to local officials
and the public if a declaration should be approved.

Introduction
The events of 2017 challenged the Federal Emergency Management Agency’s (FEMA) ability to
effectively balance concurrent large-scale disaster operations. Hurricanes Harvey, Irma, and
Maria, as well as wildfires in California, required FEMA to allocate scarce resources across four
FEMA regions, from the west coast of the United States to the Caribbean Sea. The 2017
Hurricane Season FEMA After-Action Report identified the need to create a new operational
prioritization and response construct, which would effectively characterize incidents and identify
the root causes of priority issue areas. This new construct aims to create outcome-driven,
effective solutions and help distinguish the highest priorities and most complex issues from other
incident information. In 2018, FEMA operations along the Atlantic coast, the Florida Panhandle,
Alaska, and the Western Pacific, including a historic wildfire season in California and additional
operations in Hawaii validated the pressing need for a way to prioritize response operations.
FEMA established the community lifelines during 2018 to address this need and has continued to
improve and refine its implementation based on lessons learned.  The interrelationship of ESFs,
core capabilities, and lifelines can be thought of in terms of means, ways, and ends and are
described in the following paragraphs.
Emergency Support Functions (The Means)
The Emergency Support Functions (ESF) Assistance Guide outlines specific tasks or functions
that may be carried out prior to, during and after an emergency or a disaster. The guide describes,
in specific detail, the operational system identified in the Laramie County Emergency Response
Plan (ERP), that will be used to carry out the tasks or functions listed. It should be based on the
Incident Command structure described in the basic plan.
These tasks mirror the emergency support functions of the Wyoming Response Plan and can
also be applied to specific functions within the Incident Command System (ICS) or the National
Incident Management System (NIMS).
These functions are described in a checklist format for ease in times of stress and the possibility
that a support function may be led by a person unfamiliar with the duties of the support function
or the ERP.
The Federal Government and many state governments organize their response resources and
capabilities under the ESF construct. ESFs have proven to be an effective way to bundle and
manage resources to deliver core capabilities. The Federal ESFs are the primary, but not
exclusive, Federal coordinating structures for building, sustaining, and delivering the response
core capabilities. The ESFs are vital structures for responding to Stafford Act incidents; however,
they may also be used for other incidents. Most Federal ESFs support a number of the response
core capabilities. Though not listed under every ESF, all ESFs support the core capabilities of
Planning, Operational Coordination, and Public Information and Warning.  In addition, there are
responsibilities and actions associated with Federal ESFs that extend beyond the core capabilities
and support other response activities, as well as department and agency responsibilities.
The Federal ESFs bring together the capabilities of Federal departments and agencies and other
national-level assets. ESFs are not based on the capabilities of a single department or agency,
and the functions for which they are responsible cannot be accomplished by any single
department or agency. Instead, Federal ESFs are groups of organizations that work together to
deliver core capabilities and support an effective response.

identified, assessed, and addressed.


